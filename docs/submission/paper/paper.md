# A Muse Spark 1.3 agent passes 14 of 18 held-out briefs in a live product configurator, checked by the product's rules and our code

hex-agent. Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon, Startup track, problem statement Push the Frontier. One Thousand Drones. Accurate as of 2026-11-03.

hex-agent takes a sentence and returns a build in One Thousand Drones' live Hex Cluster configurator: a share link, a parts list and a print pack made from the catalog's printable parts, a build that passes the configurator's placement and collision rules, and the model's own statement of which requirements it met, checked in code only where a brief has a coded check, by a keyword check on out-of-scope claims and, for an all-met claim, against the rules and mesh check. On the frozen agent (git tag `eval-final`), Muse Spark 1.3 through the Meta Model API passed 14 of 18 held-out briefs (77.8%) on their one pre-committed run (Wilson 95% interval 54.8 to 91.0%): 11 builds, and 3 declines of briefs written to be refused. Those passes were not audited; audits of the same checks on the development briefs (D7) and on a second held-out set (A8) found passing builds that miss a requirement no check tests, so this rate likely overstates success. An agent that leaves the build as it finds it and reports the request unmet, or declines, passes 4 of those 18. On the 36 development briefs, run five times each, it passed 173 of 180 runs (clustered 95% interval 90.7 to 100%); that figure is optimistic, because we fixed failures on those briefs. A development build cost about $0.057 of Muse Spark 1.3 tokens, an estimate at list price (no bill). A second Meta model, abstention and the earlier open-weights model are in Demo and results, and Meta models as vision judges in Use of Meta AI tooling and Demo and results, each reported apart and never pooled. Data files are labelled D1 to D8 and, for results added after the paper's first review gate, A1 to A9; H1 and H2 are hand-written notes; all are listed in the last section; short hashes name configurator releases and agent commits.

## What was built during the hackathon

Pre-existing, One Thousand Drones' product, not judged: the generation pipeline behind Hex Cluster (FreeCAD part authoring, Blender prop generation, glyph and technical-drawing generation), Hex Cluster, its [configurator](https://hex.onethousanddrones.com/), placement rules, collision check, the public `window.hexAPI`, print packs, printable parts and plant and prop models, plus the configurator releases One Thousand Drones shipped during the event (each identified by its API-contract hash in Table 1 and the report), some prompted by the agent's runs; they are product changes, excluded from the claimed work, and every result here ran on one of them. None of that source is in this repository, which holds a vendored copy of the public API contract and test fixtures.

Built during the hackathon (2026-10-27 to 2026-11-04), in this repository from its first commit (fb78560, 2026-10-27): the agent loop, browser bridge, checked tool layer, Meta model adapters, verification, honesty check, abstention, grader and rescoring, spend ledgers, evaluation suites, baselines, the replay tool and a vision judge on Meta models. Nothing was trained. Every agent result here is a Meta model. Claude (Anthropic) served as the agent's model in 1,325 debugging runs of the loop and as the reviewer agents whose verdicts are the grader audit (D7) and the demo review (D8). It was also the first advisory vision critic; Muse Spark 1.3 and Muse Wonder 1.0 now fill that role (A3), and the Claude critic rows are kept as history. HACKATHON_DELTA.md lists the pre-event product versions.

## Impact

Hex Cluster is modular 3D-printed hexagonal storage, and its existing browser-based parametric configurator already knows which parts fit where. hex-agent adds the step from a sentence such as "a patio bed for tomatoes and the plants that help them grow" to a build valid in the configurator (placement and collision rules) with a share link, a parts list, a print pack and the model's own statement of which requirements were met, partly met or not met. That is the Push the Frontier claim: in this evaluation, a manufacturing tool that took a person's clicks took their intent and returned print packs the configurator produces for builds it accepts; it is not deployed, nothing has been printed yet, and its overclaims are measured in Table 1. A development build cost about $0.057 of Muse Spark 1.3 tokens at list price (Table 1). It is built for makers who know what they want but not the part catalog. hex-agent drives the configurator from outside, through its public API, with design from a natural-language brief, evaluated experimentally as reported here; customer-facing deployment in the configurator is planned, and commercial adoption and usage metrics have not yet been established. Hex Cluster is itself one output of One Thousand Drones' existing generation pipeline (pre-existing, not judged): its parts are authored parametrically in FreeCAD by scripted pipelines, its plant and prop models are generated in Blender, and its part glyphs and technical drawings are generated from the same CAD. The pipeline is designed to go further, and these steps are designed or partly built, not demonstrated here: redefining the system's geometry (a cube or pyramid cell in place of the hexagon) with its attachment logic and tolerances, and adding new interface types with their CAD, drawings, reports, assembly guides and print orientation, registered automatically in the configurator's API, in this agent's tools and in the configurator. hex-agent takes its operations and value lists from the configurator's published API contract (a vendored copy, refreshed per release) and its parts, sockets and rule verdicts from the live configurator, so a new product family would reach the agent partly through the contract; the tool layer still hard-codes hex axial coordinates and Hex Cluster part hints (src/tools.mjs), which would have to be generated as well. That is the larger frontier, and it is untested. We expect the pattern to carry to other manufactured products whose rule engine explains its refusals; we have tested one product.

## Use of Meta AI tooling

Muse Spark 1.3 through the Meta Model API is the model of the frozen agent and of every headline result: 14 of 18 held-out briefs on the pre-committed run and, as a development-set rate, 173 of 180 runs (Table 1). It ran in Standard mode with the API's reasoning-effort parameter set to low except in the medium-effort comparison: up to the review gate, across its evaluation, held-out and smoke suites, 6,054 calls, 6,048 of them answered by `muse-spark-1.3` as named in each response (6 from the first test run predate that field), 92.7% of input tokens served from the prompt cache, 11 server errors in those D1 suites, each followed by an answered call there; median latency 4,951 ms, 95th percentile 13,798 ms (D1 `metaUse`, `hosts`). Outside those suites, Meta API errors did cut 4 demo runs, and after the gate one abstention run (A1) and one addendum run (A7). Muse Wonder 1.0, a second model on the same API, passed 17 of 18 held-out briefs on its one run (Table 1; a different model, not compared). Each model reads the build, asks where parts fit and places them through 17 tools on the frozen agent (fewer on the earliest commits) over up to 30 turns, reading each refusal and the alternatives the configurator offers. Beyond tool calling, two capabilities are measured. Self-report: every requirement marked met, partial or unmet, with all-met claims checked against the brief's coded checks, the configurator's rules and its mesh check. Abstention: declining a brief that would carry a child or hold fuel, or building only its safe part. On 12 unsafe briefs on hazards the abstention block does not name, Muse Spark 1.3 passed 27 of 36 runs with the abstention feature on and 12 of 32 with it off (per-brief means 75% and 44.4%; +30.6 points paired, 95% CI 9.5 to 51.7). That is the effect of switching the feature off, its prompt block and its declined outcome together, and not a safety result: a do-nothing agent passes 11 of the 12 unsafe briefs, beside the agent's per-brief mean of 75% (49.3 to 100%), with no interval on that comparison (Demo and results). Muse Spark 1.3 and Muse Wonder 1.0 also take a build's stills as image input on the Meta Responses API and serve as an advisory vision critic and an advisory second reviewer of the demo runs (A3, A4); both are advisory and gate nothing. As critic on 60 builds the owner had marked keep or fix (the set the Claude critics judged), flagging a build when any of the purpose, rests and pose questions says fix, Muse Spark 1.3 agreed with the owner on 66.7% and 60% of builds in two passes and Muse Wonder 1.0 on 58.3% and 60%, beside 63.3% for Claude Haiku 5.5, 70% for Claude Opus 5.5 and 70% for flagging nothing; paired with flagging nothing by build, every judge's difference lies between −11.7 and 0.0 points and every 95% interval includes 0 (A3 `minusFlagNothing`, purpose, rests and pose questions; 24 comparisons computed over four question subsets, all exploratory, chosen after seeing the data, uncorrected), so on these questions the critic is not distinguishable from flagging nothing; on all four questions Claude Haiku 5.5 (−36.7 points, 95% CI −59.9 to −13.4) and each of the two Muse Wonder 1.0 passes (−23.3, −44.4 to −2.3) agreed less often than flagging nothing. As reviewer of the 41 demo runs that ended in a build (D8 judged 45, 4 of them host cuts with no build), each Muse pass agreed with the Claude reviewers' keep-or-drop verdicts on 61 to 70.7% of runs, beside 65.9% for keeping every run. The 41 runs repeat 9 briefs, so no interval is given (fewer than 10 briefs); the runs where only the Muse pass agreed with D8 against those where only keep-every-run did were 8 and 7, 7 and 6 (Muse Spark 1.3), 10 and 12, 13 and 11 (Muse Wonder 1.0) (A4 `minusKeepEveryRun`). Agreement with D8 does not measure correctness: Muse Wonder 1.0 disagreed with D8 on all five drone v5 runs in both passes, and on the one whose stills we checked, f24f0f99, the stills bear Muse out. There Muse Wonder 1.0 listed two claims as contradicted in both its passes and Muse Spark 1.3 one of them in one of its two passes (Demo and results). Claude's remaining roles are the agent's model in development runs, the reviewer agents behind D7, D8 and the A8 audit, the earlier critic rows kept as history, and drafting help (Reproduce). The open weights of Muse Spark Open ran on three partner hosts: Baseten (`meta-models/muse-spark-open-1026`), a Modal Dedicated endpoint on 4 B300 GPUs, and Fireworks.
## Technical depth and execution

Each tool call is checked in code against the configurator's published API contract (a hash of that specification, one per release, identifies the configurator version a run used), then run through `window.hexAPI` in a real browser tab. A run passes when it ends in a build or a compromise (some requirements reported partial or unmet), the placement rules and mesh collision check pass, the print pack is produced, the share link reloads in a fresh tab with the same number of parts of each family and the same parts list, and the brief's coded checks pass; a decline that leaves the build empty needs a finish and no broken rule; the brief's own checks still apply, so a buildable brief fails a decline. A check that did not run counts as failed. A requirement with no coded check rests on the model's statement, and a "met" claim that uses listed strength, load, heat, water, UV, food-safety or material terms fails the run (a keyword check), since the configurator checks fit only. A run is overclaimed when the model reports every requirement met and a brief check, placement rule or mesh check fails, so overclaim counts are lower bounds.

A pass of a build establishes validity in the configurator: no error-severity configurator issue, a clear mesh-collision gate, a print-pack link and a share link that reloads with the same number of parts of each family and the same parts list (src/agent.mjs); a decline needs a finish and no broken rule; the brief's own checks still apply, so a buildable brief fails a decline. It also requires the brief's coded checks, which count parts of a family, drawers of any kind, parts-list lines, storeys, props (by kind and per part), soil and covers, set a ceiling on parts, and test stacks, groups joined by a connector, spike kind, open drawers, the top and bottom storey, named configurator issues, the outcome, an unmet report and requirements that must not be claimed met (src/checks.mjs). Those checks do not test where a prop sits (props rest where the configurator lets them; the checks do not test where), which way a part faces in plan, or whether one part reaches or serves another (beyond a connector joining two groups). Warning-level issues (a base over an empty cell, a loose body) still pass, and a finish reporting any requirement unmet, core or not, still passes unless the brief checks for it; strict pass does not (briefs meant to be refused in part aside). Beyond these checks, functional correctness rests on the model's own statement, and physical validity was not tested: nothing was printed.

Four agent-side fixes, made after reading failures on the development briefs, are on by default from agent 1e98dbf onward (the frozen agent, Muse Wonder and the Fireworks batch): fix 1 offers slide drawers on east- and west-facing half bases; fix 2 tells the model when a batch of operations was rolled back; fix 3 shows base groups and touching walls in the build summary; fix 4 sends an all-met finish back once when the configurator reports a rule or mesh error, a floating base, evidence citing a part that is gone, or an open face under a "closed" requirement, so overclaim counts on those agents are measured with that guard on.

The 36 development briefs are three sets (12 core, 8 hard, 16 wide) written for the event, five of them honesty traps (briefs asking for something impossible or for a property the configurator cannot check, where the right answer reports it unmet). A separate set of 6 abstention briefs holds unsafe or infeasible requests and two controls; a second set of 12 unsafe briefs on hazards the prompt's abstention block does not name, plus 6 controls meant to be built, was written by an agent session that read the abstention text, the first set, its checks and the contract (no traces) and committed (2cdac85) before any run on it; the base instructions, on in every arm, already treat load ratings and food or dishwasher safety as never met. The 18 held-out briefs were committed (76e977d) before the last agent fixes and ran only on the frozen agent; two of their themes were written with fixes 1 and 3 in view. Our grader was wrong several times; every suite is graded by the code of grader c7533b7, which is the same check code as the eval-final grader (`git diff c7533b7 0c840bc` is empty for src/checks.mjs, src/agent.mjs and bin/rescore.mjs), and the paper's held-out suites are scored as run and never rescored (report sections 2.4 and 4.3; the addendum rescores its own held-out2 suites). Intervals are 95%: clustered by brief when briefs repeat (normal critical value at 30 or more briefs, t below 30; no interval is reported for fewer than 10 briefs), and Wilson over runs for one run per brief. A comparison is paired by brief and made only between runs that match in host, model, reasoning effort, contract, page build (the hash of the configurator's script bundle), agent commit and prompt, apart from the factor compared (fixes or abstention switched on or off); the one exception is the Muse Spark 1.3 effort pair below. Reported paired comparisons: fixes off against on; abstention on against off on the second set, with a sensitivity counting cut runs as failures; Modal high against low effort; Muse Spark 1.3 medium against low (exploratory: it crosses the agent-commit key, the one exception); and, in the addendum, the tool-layer fixes. The first abstention set is compared per brief only. The report adds the overclaim pair of the Modal effort comparison and six exploratory batch pairs (D2 `batchPairs`, report section 3.5, uncorrected for multiple comparisons; the two pairs that include the 10-31 batch, pooled across inferred agent commits, are not reported); pairs that cross the comparison key (D1 `effortPaired.modalHighMinusLowPooledLowVariant` and `basetenHighMinusLow`, D2 `effortPooledLow`, `abstention`) are not reported. The smallest resolvable difference is the true difference a pair would detect with 80% power.

Who decides what in a run (src/agent.mjs, src/tools.mjs):

| Who | Decides or does |
|---|---|
| Muse model | every tool call and its arguments; undo; whether to take an offered alternative; each requirement's status, evidence and outcome |
| Tool layer (built in the event) | resolves sockets and contract values; helpers choose surfaces, plants the model names (or the configurator's pick) and fills, re-send the configurator's suggested fix once, unasked, and re-send props refused BUSY after the settle; re-runs a batch the configurator rolled back only for refusals saying the state already holds; limits of 30 turns, 30 operations a call, 250 a run, and a $3 cost cap per run in suites |
| Configurator (pre-existing) | accepts or refuses each operation with a code, alternatives and a suggested fix; rolls back an atomic batch whole; placement rules and mesh check |
| Code at finish | rules, mesh, print pack, share-link reload, brief checks, out-of-scope keyword scan; sends an all-met finish back once on an error-severity rule, a mesh clash, a floating base, evidence citing a part that is gone, or an open face under a "closed" requirement |
| The model's word only | that every requirement is met; facing in plan, visibility, and most of what rests on what |

## Limitations and what was not run

- Development-set rates for the 36 briefs: briefs, checks and guards changed after we read failures on them; four of the 36 (load-rating, dishwasher-safe and both prompt-injection briefs) are named or near-quoted in the prompt, a fifth (minimal-bin) is anticipated by it, and five of the six abstention briefs are named in it (the second abstention set's hazards are not in its abstention block, though the base instructions name load ratings and food safety, A1). The held-out set ran once pre-committed, then four more times after that result was seen, and two of its themes were written with fixes in view.
- One product and our own grader, reviewed by no third party. The audit found false passes on 4 of 36 audited briefs, all on relations between parts; the checks count parts and plants, not what a viewer sees. The checks also cannot see which way a build faces or what the default view shows: in the demo runs the reviewers found tall rows hiding low ones (all 5 runs of the first terrace brief), a multimeter on end in all 5 passing drone runs, drawers facing away in 3 of 5 desk runs and tweezers on drawer rims, several under reports that called the requirement met, while every brief check passed (D8, hand-tabulated; report section 3.4), and the drone run shown makes two claims its stills contradict (Demo and results). In all five drone runs the multimeter was refused for overlapping the turned quad; the dress helper then re-sent the configurator's suggested fix on its own, which stood it on end (src/tools.mjs). The model did not choose that orientation; it kept it and reported the row met.
- Grader audits read passes only and were not blinded: D7 covered development briefs, A8 the addendum's held-out2, and the first held-out set's passes were not audited.
- No architecture baseline was run. The do-nothing agent is a floor and ablates nothing: no Meta run used the same configurator API with a minimal tool layer (no contract checks, no relaying of the configurator's refusal alternatives, no verify step or honesty check). The frozen-agent ablations switch off fixes 1 to 4 (13 briefs, the fixes-on arm at ceiling) or abstention, and the addendum adds six tool-layer fixes (A7); none removes the layer, so what the tool layer as a whole adds beyond the model and the configurator is not measured.
- No build was printed or assembled in this evaluation; "valid" means valid in the configurator.
- Muse Wonder 1.0's held-out rate is one run per brief, a descriptive rate. Muse Spark Open and Muse Spark 1.3 differ in model, host, release and agent commit, so no difference between them is claimed.
