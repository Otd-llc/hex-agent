# A Muse Spark 1.3 agent passes 14 of 18 held-out briefs in a live product configurator, checked by the product's rules and our code

hex-agent. Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon, Startup track, problem statement Push the Frontier. One Thousand Drones. Accurate as of 2026-11-02.

hex-agent takes a sentence and returns a build in One Thousand Drones' live Hex Cluster configurator: a share link, a parts list and a print pack made from the catalog's printable parts, a build that passes the configurator's placement and collision rules, and a statement of which requirements were met. On the frozen agent (git tag `eval-final`), Muse Spark 1.3 through the Meta Model API passed 14 of 18 held-out briefs (77.8%) on their one pre-committed run (Wilson 95% interval 54.8 to 91.0%): 11 builds, and 3 declines of briefs written to be refused. An agent that leaves the build as it finds it and reports the request unmet, or declines, passes 4 of those 18. On the 36 development briefs, run five times each, it passed 173 of 180 runs (clustered 95% interval 90.7 to 100%); that figure is optimistic, because we fixed failures on those briefs. Data files are labelled D1 to D8 and listed in the last section; short hashes name configurator releases and agent commits. Earlier, in seven effort-low, five-repeat batches that differ in host, configurator release and agent commit, Meta's open-weights Muse Spark Open passed 89.4 to 93.9% of runs on the development briefs; the models are reported apart and never pooled.

## What was built during the hackathon

Pre-existing, One Thousand Drones' product, not judged: the generation pipeline behind Hex Cluster (FreeCAD part authoring, Blender prop generation, glyph and technical-drawing generation), Hex Cluster, its [configurator](https://hex.onethousanddrones.com/), placement rules, collision check, the public `window.hexAPI`, print packs, printable parts and plant and prop models, plus the configurator releases One Thousand Drones shipped during the event (each identified by its API-contract hash in Tables 1 and 2), some prompted by the agent's runs; they are product changes, excluded from the claimed work, and every result here ran on one of them. None of that source is in this repository, which holds a vendored copy of the public API contract and test fixtures.

Built during the hackathon, in this repository from its first commit (fb78560, 2026-10-27): the agent loop, browser bridge, checked tool layer, Meta model adapters, verification, honesty check, abstention, grader and rescoring, spend ledgers, evaluation suites, baselines and the replay tool. Nothing was trained. Claude (Anthropic) served as the agent's model in 1,325 debugging runs of the loop, as an advisory vision critic that gates nothing, and as the reviewer agents that judged the grader audit (D7) and the demo runs (D8); every agent result here is a Meta model. ## Impact

Hex Cluster is modular 3D-printed hexagonal storage, and its existing browser-based parametric configurator already knows which parts fit where. hex-agent adds the step from a sentence such as "a patio bed for tomatoes and the plants that help them grow" to a valid build with a share link, a parts list, a print pack and a statement of which requirements were met, partly met or not met. That is the Push the Frontier claim: a manufacturing tool that took a person's clicks now takes their intent and returns a print pack the configurator accepts; nothing has been printed yet, and its overclaims are measured in Table 1. A development build cost about $0.057 of Muse Spark 1.3 tokens at list price (Table 1). It is built for makers who know what they want but not the part catalog. hex-agent extends the configurator with natural-language autonomous design, evaluated experimentally as reported here; customer-facing deployment in the configurator is planned, and commercial adoption and usage metrics have not yet been established. Hex Cluster is itself one output of One Thousand Drones' existing generation pipeline (pre-existing, not judged): its parts are authored parametrically in FreeCAD by scripted pipelines, its plant and prop models are generated in Blender, and its part glyphs and technical drawings are generated from the same CAD. The pipeline is designed to go further, and these steps are designed or partly built, not demonstrated here: redefining the system's geometry (a cube or pyramid cell in place of the hexagon) with its attachment logic and tolerances, and adding new interface types with their CAD, drawings, reports, assembly guides and print orientation, registered automatically in the configurator's API, in this agent's tools and in the configurator. Because hex-agent takes its operations and value lists from the configurator's published API contract, and its parts, sockets and rule verdicts from the live configurator, a product family the pipeline generates is one the agent can design with. That is the larger frontier; beyond Hex Cluster it is untested. We expect the pattern to carry to other manufactured products whose rule engine explains its refusals; we have tested one product.

## Use of Meta AI tooling

Three Meta models do the design work, measured apart: Muse Spark 1.3 and Muse Wonder 1.0 on the Meta Model API, and the open-weights Muse Spark Open on partner hosts. Each model reads the build, asks where parts fit and places them through 17 tools over up to 30 turns, reading each refusal and the alternatives the configurator offers. Beyond tool calling, two capabilities are measured: self-report (every requirement marked met, partial or unmet, with all-met claims checked) and abstention (declining a brief that would carry a child or hold fuel, or building only its safe part): with abstention on, the six abstention briefs (four unsafe or infeasible, two controls) passed 18 of 18 runs, against 10 of 18 with it off, on briefs the abstention prompt mostly names (below). Muse Spark 1.3 ran through the Meta Model API (Standard mode, the API's reasoning-effort parameter set to low except in the medium-effort comparison): across its evaluation, held-out and smoke suites, 6,054 calls, 6,048 of them answered by `muse-spark-1.3` as named in each response (6 from the first test run predate that field), 92.7% of input tokens served from the prompt cache, 11 server errors, each followed by an answered call; median latency 4,951 ms, 95th percentile 13,798 ms (D1 `metaUse`, `hosts`). Muse Wonder 1.0, a second model on the same API, ran the 36 development briefs once each. The open weights of Muse Spark Open ran on three partner hosts: Baseten (`meta-models/muse-spark-open-1026`), a Modal Dedicated endpoint on 4 B300 GPUs, and Fireworks.
## Technical depth and execution

Each tool call is checked in code against the configurator's published API contract (a hash of that specification, one per release, identifies the configurator version a run used), then run through `window.hexAPI` in a real browser tab. A run passes when it ends in a build or a compromise (some requirements reported partial or unmet), the placement rules and mesh collision check pass, the print pack is produced, the share link reloads in a fresh tab with the same number of parts of each family and the same parts list, and the brief's coded checks pass; a decline that leaves the build empty needs only a finish and no broken rule on the configurator's side; the brief's checks still apply, and a buildable brief fails a decline. A check that did not run counts as failed. A requirement with no coded check rests on the model's statement, and a "met" claim that uses listed strength, heat, water, food-safety or material terms fails the run (a keyword check), since the configurator checks fit only. A run is overclaimed when the model reports every requirement met and a brief check, placement rule or mesh check fails, so overclaim counts are lower bounds.

Four agent-side fixes, made after reading failures on the development briefs, are on by default from agent 1e98dbf onward (the frozen agent, Muse Wonder and the Fireworks row): fix 1 offers slide drawers on east- and west-facing half bases; fix 2 tells the model when a batch of operations was rolled back; fix 3 shows base groups and touching walls in the build summary; fix 4 sends an all-met finish back once when the configurator reports a rule or mesh error, a floating base, evidence citing a part that is gone, or an open face under a "closed" requirement, so overclaim counts on those agents are measured with that guard on.

The 36 development briefs are three sets (12 core, 8 hard, 16 wide) written for the event, five of them honesty traps (briefs asking for something impossible or for a property the configurator cannot check, where the right answer reports it unmet). A separate set of 6 abstention briefs holds unsafe or infeasible requests and two controls. The 18 held-out briefs were committed (76e977d) before the last agent fixes and ran only on the frozen agent; two of their themes were written with fixes 1 and 3 in view. Our grader was wrong several times (report section 4.3); development suites run before the Meta Model API were rescored from their traces with grader c7533b7, first scores kept beside them. Later Meta suites were graded by code unchanged since c7533b7: the first was rescored at 56ef72c, the rest scored as run. The held-out suites are scored as run and never rescored. Intervals are 95%: clustered by brief when briefs repeat (normal critical value at 30 or more briefs, t below 30; no interval is reported for fewer than 10 briefs), and Wilson over runs for one run per brief. A comparison is paired by brief and made only between runs that match in host, model, reasoning effort, contract, page build (the hash of the configurator's script bundle), agent commit and prompt, apart from the factor compared (fixes or abstention switched on or off); the one exception is the Muse Spark 1.3 effort pair below. Three paired differences and one per-brief comparison are reported; further exploratory pairs (D1 `effortPaired.modalHighMinusLowPooledLowVariant` and `basetenHighMinusLow`, D2 `batchPairs`, `effortPooledLow`, `abstention`) are not. The smallest resolvable difference is the true difference a pair would detect with 80% power.

## Limitations and what was not run

- Development-set rates for the 36 briefs: briefs, checks and guards changed after we read failures on them; four of the 36 (load-rating, dishwasher-safe and both prompt-injection briefs) are named or near-quoted in the prompt, a fifth (minimal-bin) is anticipated by it, and five of the six abstention briefs are named in it. The held-out set ran once pre-committed, then four more times after that result was seen, and two of its themes were written with fixes in view.
- One product and our own grader, reviewed by no third party. The audit found false passes on 4 of 36 audited briefs, all on relations between parts; the checks count parts and plants, not what a viewer sees. The checks also cannot see which way a build faces: in 4 of the first 15 demo runs, and in all 5 runs of the first terrace brief, the tall row stood nearest the configurator's default camera and hid the low rows; several of those reports marked the row order as met although the run's own look reported plants hidden, and every coded check passed. Reviewing the workbenches found more claims the checks cannot test: in all 5 passing drone runs, including the one shown, the multimeter stands on end with its display facing away, unreported, while the reports mark the quad's arms as clear of its neighbour; in 3 of the 5 desk runs the drawers face away from the viewer while the reports say they face it; in the printing station's second version the dress tool put tweezers on the drawer rims while 3 reports said they were inside, so the third version dropped them; and every printing run adds two stray rod stubs (D8, hand-tabulated).
- No build was printed or assembled in this evaluation; "valid" means valid in the configurator.
- Muse Wonder 1.0 ran once per brief, a descriptive rate. Muse Spark Open and Muse Spark 1.3 differ in model, host, release and agent commit, so no difference between them is claimed.
- The demo traces record the tree as dirty: two reporting files (not agent code) were edited after the demo suite loaded its modules; the agent code that ran is identical to `eval-final`.
